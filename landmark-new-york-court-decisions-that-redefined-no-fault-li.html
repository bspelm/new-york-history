Landmark New York Court Decisions That Redefined No-Fault Liability

Four decades of Court of Appeals rulings on serious injury thresholds, staged-collision fraud, and provider standing have quietly rewritten Article 51. A veteran defense litigator walks through the cases that mattered.

A conversation with MARIANNE ORTIZ-KRELL · Recorded in Brooklyn Heights · Edited for length

The New York no-fault statute, on paper, looks tidy. In practice, thirty-plus years of appellate opinions have layered on so much judicial gloss that first-year associates now spend weeks just learning what the phrase "serious injury" means before they can even open a file. When a fender-bender client asks the classic entry question, if someone rear-ends you whose insurance do you call, the honest answer sends them into a labyrinth built not by the legislature but by the Court of Appeals. Marianne Ortiz-Krell has litigated no-fault matters since 1998, defending both carriers and, occasionally, medical providers caught up in verification disputes. We sat down over tea to sketch the case-law arc.

Everyone starts with Licari v. Elliott. Why does that 1982 opinion still frame the conversation?

Licari is the ur-text. Judge Jasen writing for a unanimous court held that the "serious injury" threshold in Insurance Law § 5102(d) is a question of law for the trial court in the first instance, and he essentially told the trial bench: do your job as a gatekeeper. Before Licari, judges were reflexively sending every soft-tissue case to a jury. After Licari, a defendant with a decent orthopedist could get summary judgment. That single procedural pivot reshaped the entire economics of the plaintiffs' bar.

And Toure v. Avis in 2002?

Toure was the counter-swing. By the late 1990s the trial courts had gotten very aggressive with summary judgment, and plaintiffs' lawyers were losing cases that arguably deserved a jury. Judge Ciparick's opinion in Toure reminded everyone that a "significant limitation of use" and a "permanent consequential limitation" can be established through either quantitative range-of-motion measurements or a qualitative assessment by a physician, provided that qualitative assessment has an objective basis. It rehabilitated the plaintiff's toolkit.

Ten cases in and every practitioner mentions Pommells. What made it a hinge point?

Pommells v. Perez, decided in April 2005, is the case that put the "gap in treatment" defense on the map. The Court of Appeals consolidated three appeals and held that an unexplained cessation of treatment can defeat a plaintiff's serious-injury claim on summary judgment. Overnight, every defense examination under oath started with, "Tell me about the eight months between your last physical therapy visit and today." It also introduced the "intervening cause" analysis for degenerative conditions, which is why every defense IME report now includes a paragraph on preexisting spondylosis.

"The statute the Assembly passed in 1973 and the statute we actually litigate in 2024 are, functionally, different documents. The difference is written in Court of Appeals slip opinions." — Ortiz-Krell

Let's talk about the fraud side. Mallela in 2005 was seismic.

It was. State Farm v. Mallela came up on a certified question from the Second Circuit, and the Court of Appeals held that a professional corporation that is fraudulently incorporated, meaning owned or controlled by non-physicians in violation of Business Corporation Law and Education Law, is not entitled to reimbursement of no-fault benefits. That opinion single-handedly killed the layperson-owned clinic model that had metastasized across Queens and Brooklyn in the late 1990s. Every carrier's Special Investigations Unit reorganized around Mallela discovery inside of a year. The New York Times ran a lengthy piece in September 2003 chronicling the staged-collision rings that had been draining the system, and Mallela gave carriers the doctrinal tool to actually claw money back.

And the Countrywide line of cases?

Countrywide Insurance v. Preferred Trucking and its progeny locked down the timeliness rules. Under 11 NYCRR 65-3.5 and 65-3.8, a carrier must either pay or deny within 30 days, and if it wants to invoke a founded-belief-of-fraud defense it must scrupulously follow the verification protocol. The Court of Appeals has been unforgiving to carriers who miss deadlines. A perfectly meritorious fraud defense evaporates if the denial goes out on day 31. That tension, substantive fraud policing versus procedural rigidity, is the great unresolved puzzle of modern no-fault practice.

Where does Perl v. Meher fit?

Perl, decided in November 2011, was Judge Smith at his most pragmatic. The Second Department had developed a rule that plaintiffs' physicians had to produce contemporaneous quantitative measurements taken shortly after the accident. If those measurements were missing, the case died at summary judgment. Perl rejected that rigidity, holding that contemporaneous quantitative measurements are not indispensable so long as the plaintiff can prove serious injury through other competent evidence. It restored a lot of cases that would have been dismissed under the Second Department's stricter view. You can read the opinion on the state's public reporter at the Court of Appeals decisions archive, and it's worth reading in full because Judge Smith is candid about why the earlier line of Appellate Division cases had drifted too far.

If a first-year associate asked you what the through-line is, what would you say?

I'd say the statute the Assembly passed in 1973 was a compromise. The courts have been renegotiating that compromise ever since, sometimes tilting toward claimants, sometimes toward carriers, but always adding texture the drafters never anticipated. Article 51 today is 90% statute and 10% Court of Appeals gloss by volume, but 50/50 by practical effect. You cannot litigate a threshold motion or a provider reimbursement dispute in 2024 without knowing Licari, Toure, Pommells, Perl, and Mallela cold. If you skip any one of them, you will be embarrassed at oral argument.

Last question. What case is the next hinge?

Keep an eye on the pending disputes over telemedicine billing under the amended regulations, and on the ongoing appellate treatment of arbitration awards under Insurance Law § 5106(c). The Second Department has been reviewing an unusual number of master-arbitrator vacaturs in the last two terms. Somewhere in that pipeline is the next Pommells. I don't know which docket number it will carry, but I'd bet the decision drops before 2027.
