Gilded Age Divorce Scandals & the Roots of New York's Fault Era

For one hundred and forty-three years, New York State maintained the strictest divorce regime in the union, and for one hundred and forty-three years, the families with the most to lose under it spent enormous sums proving exactly why it could not last. The story of how the Vanderbilts, Astors, Goelets and Belmonts hauled their marital wreckage across state lines, through tabloid front pages, and eventually into the joint legislative record at Albany is a cultural history more than a legal one, and it is the precondition for everything that followed, including the modern bench of practitioners now ranked among the best high net worth divorce lawyers working in Manhattan today. The statute on the books from 1787 onward permitted divorce on a single ground: adultery. Anything else, including cruelty, abandonment, and what one would now call coercive control, was a problem for the priest, the press, or the porter loading trunks for the night train to Reno.

This zine is not about the statute. It is about the scandals that ate around it like rust, hollowing it from the inside until the legislature finally, in August 2010, signed Domestic Relations Law §170(7) and ended the fault-only era. The people in these pages did not set out to reform anything. They set out to live their lives, and the gap between their lives and the law's permissible exits became, over four generations, the most reliable beat in American journalism.

"No divorce in New York is ever really about adultery. It is about who agrees to be caught."
PG. 02

The Vanderbilt Problem, 1895

Begin with Alva Erskine Smith Vanderbilt, who in March 1895 obtained a New York divorce from William Kissam Vanderbilt on grounds of adultery. The grounds were real. They were also performed. William, by then deep into his affair with Nellie Neustretter, consented to be the named party so that Alva could exit the marriage with her standing intact and a settlement reported in The New York Times at the time as among the largest in American history. Eight months later, Alva married Oliver Belmont. Adultery Eleven weeks after that, her daughter Consuelo was walked, weeping, down the aisle at St. Thomas Church on Fifth Avenue and into a forced marriage with the ninth Duke of Marlborough. The wider arc is sketched in the public record of the Alva Belmont biography, which doubles as a primer on how Gilded Age women learned to use the divorce statute as both weapon and exit door.

The Vanderbilt divorce taught the Four Hundred a lesson the Hewitts and the Stuyvesants had only suspected: a New York divorce was not a finding of fact. It was a piece of theater staged for the County Clerk, with a script agreed upon by the parties' counsel weeks in advance. The "co-respondent" was sometimes the genuine paramour, sometimes a paid actress hired to be discovered at a Murray Hill hotel by a photographer working on retainer. Newspapers called the latter arrangement "collusive adultery," and by 1920 the New York County Bar was openly debating whether the practice had so corrupted the matrimonial calendar that the entire regime had to be scrapped.

The Raid Hotel

By 1922 there were five Manhattan hotels widely known among matrimonial lawyers as reliable venues for staged "discoveries." Cash on the dresser. A photographer in the hall. The wife's investigator a hired actor.

The Paid Co-Respondent

Rates in 1925 were reported at $50 to $100 per evening. A 1934 grand jury heard testimony from a Brooklyn woman who claimed she had been hired as a co-respondent in forty-three separate divorce actions.

PG. 03

Newport, Saratoga, and the Migratory Marriage

The summer migration of the Astor and Goelet households to Bellevue Avenue had less to do with sea air than with jurisdictional planning. By 1898 Rhode Island permitted divorce for "gross misbehavior and wickedness repugnant to and in violation of the marriage covenant," language elastic enough to fit almost any miserable arrangement. A New York socialite who established Newport residency in May could be free by Labor Day. Saratoga Springs offered a similar utility during racing season for those who preferred the upstate scene.

What made the Newport route distinct was its respectability. No tabloid raid, no paid actress, no perjured affidavit. The wife took a cottage on Ochre Point, the children's tutor was retained for the summer, and by the time the household returned to East 72nd Street in October, the matter was settled. New York courts generally honored these decrees under the Full Faith and Credit Clause, though there were exceptions See Williams v. NC that gave matrimonial lawyers steady work into the 1940s.

The Reno Six-Week Cure

The migration that defined the cultural imagination, however, was westward. Nevada's six-week residency requirement, adopted in 1931, turned a railroad town into the divorce capital of America. The Riverside Hotel on South Virginia Street kept a permanent suite for clients of New York counsel. The Pyramid Lake ranches advertised in Town & Country. A woman of means could arrive on the City of San Francisco at the end of January, take the cure, and be back at the Colony Club for the spring season with a Nevada decree in her clutch bag.

SIX WEEKS · ONE TRAIN · A NEW LIFE

The pattern was so well established that The New Yorker ran a 1937 piece by Janet Flanner describing the "Reno colony" as essentially a Murray Hill book club relocated to the Truckee River for the summer. Husbands flew out for the day of the hearing. Lawyers commuted by Pullman. The fiction that any of these parties had become Nevada residents in any meaningful sense was understood by everyone, including the Washoe County judges, and accepted as a humane workaround for a New York statute that the rest of the country found embarrassing.

PG. 04

Why Albany Held the Line

If the system was so transparently broken, why did it persist? The answer is partly the Catholic Conference, partly Tammany, partly inertia, and partly the fact that the people with the resources to evade the statute were also the people with the political access to change it, and they did not particularly want to. A collusive Manhattan divorce or a discreet Reno trip preserved reputations in a way that an honest no-fault filing never could. The statute's cruelty fell, as it almost always did, on people who could not afford railroad fare to Nevada.

The 1966 reform, which added cruelty, abandonment, imprisonment, and separation to the grounds for divorce, was a half-measure passed under pressure from Governor Rockefeller after a state commission documented the perjury epidemic in plain language. The full record of those hearings remains a worthwhile read for anyone interested in how legal regimes survive long after their public legitimacy is gone; significant portions are preserved through the New York State Unified Court System's historical archives.

  • 1787 New York adopts adultery as the sole ground for divorce.
  • 1895 Alva Vanderbilt divorce; tabloid template established.
  • 1931 Nevada cuts residency to six weeks. Reno becomes an industry.
  • 1934 Brooklyn grand jury exposes "professional co-respondent" trade.
  • 1966 Divorce Reform Law adds non-adultery grounds.
  • 1980 Equitable distribution replaces title-based property regime.
  • 2010 DRL §170(7) signed; New York becomes the final no-fault state.
PG. 05

The Cultural Residue

What the scandals left behind, beyond the case law, was a particular kind of public literacy about marriage among wealthy New Yorkers. Generations grew up reading about the Mdivani brothers, the Hutton heiress, the various Mrs. Astors, Brenda Frazier's debutante season collapsing into divorce court within four years, and they learned that the marital contract among people of property was a working document, periodically renegotiated, and that the law's pretense otherwise was a fiction maintained for the benefit of the clergy and the columnists.

That literacy survives. It is why prenuptial agreements among Manhattan couples have an acceptance rate that would have shocked an earlier generation. It is why the equitable distribution bar developed, in the decades after 1980, a culture of forensic accounting and asset tracing that has few parallels in American matrimonial practice. And it is why, when the legislature finally moved in 2010, the change was almost anticlimactic. The cultural reform had been completed a century earlier in the parlors of Newport and the lobby of the Riverside Hotel. The statute simply caught up.

The statute caught up. It did not lead.

The reading list below is partial. The full record runs through the society pages of the Herald Tribune, the divorce calendars of the Supreme Court for New York County, and the privately printed memoirs that occasionally surface at the Strand. The story is not finished. Every reform creates the conditions for the next round of evasions, and the matrimonial bar of 2025 is already arguing about cryptocurrency the way the bar of 1925 argued about Nevada residency. The names change. The script does not.
